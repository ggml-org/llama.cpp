# Talker resynthesis as a consent lane for Route B

Deep-research record, 2026-08-05. Commissioned by the architect to test the
Talker-as-anonymizer direction for Route B voice-bearing pairs. Verdict
landed; feeds docs/tessera-s2s-design.md section 8. 41 sources; primary
legal texts verified against EDPB originals.

## Verdict

The proposed lane - transcribe user speech on-device, re-synthesize the transcript with the Talker in a fixed preset voice, retain the `(text, codes)` pair, discard the original audio - is viable, but only under a precise name. It releases the voice from the pair; it does not anonymize the pair. The acoustic channel is severed by construction, and the evidence that text-bottleneck resynthesis is the effective class of voice anonymization is now strong. The linguistic channel is untouched: the retained text is still the user's actual utterance, and linguistic style is a measured, exploitable biometric. Under the EDPB's current anonymization framework, the lane therefore produces anonymous data only if the content layer is separately transformed and evaluated before anything is retained, trained on, or shipped [1][2].

Three judgments carry the rest of this report:

1. The Talker clears the acoustic channel not by statistical luck but by architecture. In the hardest public evaluation (VoicePrivacy 2024, semi-informed attacker who retrains speaker verification on the anonymized corpus itself), cascaded ASR->TTS systems reached near-chance privacy scores (EER ~ 48%, where 50% is chance), while voice conversion systems that condition directly on the user's audio leaked speaker identity badly (EER 8-20%) [3][4]. The Talker variant is strictly stronger than any evaluated cascade, because no prosodic or acoustic feature of the input audio conditions the synthesis at all.
2. The Talker clears nothing in the content channel. Author-attribution models re-identify speakers from transcripts alone with macro-F1 above 0.87-0.90, from as few as five transcripts in open-set conditions; LLM agents now re-identify anonymous writers at 25-67% recall against public records [5][6]. A retained `(text, codes)` pair is pseudonymous at best, and it is the text that identifies, not the codes.
3. The correct legal characterization is biometric-modality removal plus content transformation, and the whole chain - capture, transcription, transformation, resynthesis, pooling, egress - is regulated processing of personal data that needs a lawful basis, disclosure at collection time, and retained documentation, regardless of how anonymous the final artifact is [1][2].

## Two identity channels in a voice-bearing pair

Every voice-bearing record contains two independent identity channels. The acoustic channel is the biometric signal: timbre, fundamental frequency, rhythm, and the speaker-specific habits that speaker-verification systems exploit, and that laws like the Illinois BIPA capture under the term "voiceprint". The linguistic channel is what was said and how it was said: the propositional content, plus the idiolect - vocabulary, syntax, discourse habits - that persists even when punctuation and capitalization are stripped [7][8].

De-identifying one channel does nothing to the other. The VoicePrivacy Challenge makes this structural: its best anonymization systems are required to preserve linguistic content, and the evaluation measures it as word error rate of an ASR system listening to the anonymized audio - the best systems land at 2.37% WER against 1.85% on unprocessed speech, meaning the anonymized voice still carries a nearly perfect transcript of everything the speaker said [3]. Any consent lane built on voice transformation therefore has to answer two separate questions: is the voice gone, and are the words handled? The Talker proposal answers the first definitively and the second not at all.

## Acoustic channel of text-bottleneck resynthesis

VoicePrivacy 2024 evaluated anonymization against a semi-informed attacker: the attacker knows the anonymization system, anonymizes enrollment utterances with it, retrains an automatic speaker verification (ASV) model on anonymized data labeled with the original speakers' identities, and then tries to re-identify each anonymized trial utterance [3]. Against that adversary, the results split cleanly by architecture. Cascaded systems that transcribe with ASR and re-synthesize with TTS reached EER ~ 48% - effectively chance [3]; a Whisper-VITS cascade averaged 48.25% EER [4]. Voice conversion systems, which transform the user's audio directly, leaked: kNN-VC averaged 7.95% EER in one evaluation and around 15% in another; ConVec2Mel-VC 9.7% [4][9]. The JHU team's diagnosis is the load-bearing finding for this lane: prosody replication from the source utterance is what leaks identity through VC pipelines, and replacing the speaker footprint with multi-speaker TTS is what eliminates it - "TTS methods perform better at anonymization" precisely because the text bottleneck discards the speaker's acoustic signal [4].

The residual channels that survive even good anonymization are measured, and they matter for the Talker's design constraints. Prosody encodes identity: phone duration and variation alone let the VPC attacker identify speakers, and anonymizing those two prosodic factors significantly raises privacy [10]. Fundamental frequency and linguistic features re-entangle with the speaker embedding at vocoding time in VC systems [11]. Emotion is a partial identity proxy too - a speaker-verification model trained only on emotion embeddings re-identified speakers at 19.28% EER on one test set and below 10% on others, and the challenge's own results show privacy and emotion preservation are in tension [9]. These findings are exactly why the Talker's construction is stronger than anything VoicePrivacy evaluated: the best cascades still pass prosodic features through the bottleneck, while the Talker conditions code generation only on text and a fixed preset voice embedding. There is no F0 path, no duration path, no emotion path from the user's audio into the retained codes, because there is no path at all. The decoded waveform through Code2Wav is the preset voice speaking the text, not a transformation of the user's voice.

Two supporting results round out the acoustic picture. Quantized semantic and acoustic tokens act as an effective bottleneck on speaker information in codec-based anonymization, which is relevant because the Talker family is a codec language model generating exactly such tokens [11]. And the differential-privacy speaker-anonymization literature concludes that disentanglement of identity from content and prosody is never perfect - linguistic and prosodic attributes still contain speaker information unless explicitly sanitized [12]. The narrowest available bottleneck is text, and that is the bottleneck this lane uses.

One structural wall follows directly: the presets-only decision is load-bearing. The moment reference-audio conditioning returns - voice cloning, voice design from a sample, prosody transfer from the input - the lane reopens precisely the channel the VC literature shows to be leaky [4][9][10]. Route B data generation must forbid the reference-audio path as a hard constraint, not a product default.

## Content channel of retained transcripts

The transcript is the pair's remaining identity channel, and the evidence that transcripts identify is now dense across methods and eras.

On speaker attribution from transcripts specifically: an Aalto University evaluation treating state-of-the-art author-attribution models as adversaries found closed-set macro-F1 above 0.90 on topic-consistent datasets, and open-set few-shot re-identification of unseen speakers from as few as five transcripts peaking at 0.87 macro-F1; the linguistic fingerprint decomposes into topic and style, with style as the stable cue [5]. A TACL benchmark confirms attribution models transfer to conversational speech transcripts [13]. Stylometric attribution works even on normalized transcripts with punctuation and capitalization removed, driven mainly by token n-grams [14]. And transcription errors do not protect: attribution on errorful ASR output performs as well as or better than on human transcripts (up to 0.88 AUC), plausibly because ASR errors themselves capture speaker-specific features [15].

On LLM-era re-identification: GPT-4 performs zero-shot named speaker identification from dialogue transcripts, raising recall from 52% (audio model) to 98% on Estonian radio talk shows where speakers are introduced by name [16]. A 2026 ETH/Anthropic study re-identified anonymous forum users from writing alone at 25-67% recall with 70-90% precision, and up to 45% recall at 99% precision linking Hacker News users to LinkedIn profiles; success scales with the amount of text available per person [6]. Agentic inference-driven linkage reconstructs identities from individually non-identifying cues - 79.2% on the Netflix-prize setting versus 56.0% for classical matching - and does so even during benign tasks [17].

The pre-LLM canon points the same direction. AOL's 2006 release of 20 million search queries from 650,000 users replaced user IDs with numeric pseudonyms and left the query text untouched; New York Times reporters identified user No. 4417749 within days from the content of her queries [18][19]. A systematic review of re-identification attacks across health-data studies found an average re-identification rate of roughly 26%, and 34% for health data specifically [20]. The lesson is uniform: removing keys does not anonymize content, because content is the identifier.

The constructive findings matter as much as the attacks. Named-entity masking alone does not provide meaningful protection, but paraphrasing with an LLM - which alters style, not just entities - reduced adversary performance by more than 50% on conversational data [5]. In the ASR-TTS pipeline setting specifically, contextual rewriting of transcripts to eliminate speaker-specific style while preserving meaning is an effective defense against content-only attackers, and the authors recommend it as a required step for long-form audio [7]. That is the transformation the retained text must pass through before the pair is kept - and because the Talker synthesizes from the transformed text, the `(text, codes)` pair stays aligned for Route B training after the transformation. The anonymizer and the training-data generator are the same pipeline stage.

## Grading against the EDPB three-criterion test

The governing framework changed materially in July 2026. EDPB Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation, adopted on 7 July 2026 for public consultation open until 30 October 2026, replace the 2014 Article 29 Working Party Opinion 05/2014 [1][2][21]. Two features define the analysis. First, the relative approach endorsed by the CJEU in EDPS v SRB: anonymity is assessed from the perspective of each entity that may come into contact with the data, so the same dataset can be personal data for the app vendor and anonymous for a keyless public recipient [1][21]. Second, a three-criterion test in renamed form: No Record Isolation (no unique combination of attribute values relating to a single individual), No Linkage (no record linkable to other data about the same individual), and No Inference (no specific and meaningful inference about an individual that relies on the given data rather than general knowledge) [1]. All three passed means the data "can safely be considered anonymous"; a failure does not automatically make data personal but requires further analysis, ideally with safety margins and periodic reassessment [1][21].

Graded against those criteria, the lane's artifacts split sharply. The acoustic side - preset-voice codes with the original audio destroyed - passes by construction under condition C2 below: no record isolation, because the codes carry no attributable combination of the user's attributes; no linkage, because no acoustic relationship to the user's voice remains to match against enrollment audio; no inference, because decoding yields the preset voice, not information about the speaker. The raw transcript side fails all three: a person's unrevised phrasing is a unique attribute combination; style links their records across batches and to external text; and the content supports specific, meaningful inferences about health, finances, relationships - precisely the inferences the guidelines define as violating the criterion [1]. The transformed-text side - scrubbed, paraphrased, adversarially evaluated - is where the lane either earns anonymity or documents residual risk; that is the "further analysis" posture the guidelines prescribe, not a weakness in the design [1].

Two framework points bear directly on the models themselves. The guidelines name extraction attacks against "supposedly-anonymous AI models" among the techniques that can violate anonymity, and state explicitly that AI models and synthetic datasets can violate No Inference when querying or prompting them with additional information elicits specific and meaningful information about an individual [1]. EDPB Opinion 28/2024 on AI models sets the matching test: a model is anonymous only if both the likelihood of direct (including probabilistic) extraction of training personal data and the likelihood of obtaining such data from queries are insignificant [22]. A drafter or TTS model trained on retained pairs is therefore a second retention site: if it can be prompted to regurgitate a user's utterance, the lane fails No Inference through the model even if the dataset itself passed.

On synthetic-data status, the lane's pairs are synthetic in the codes but conditioned per-record on personal text, and the regulator tests resolve the status by inferrability rather than origin. The ICO position is that synthetic data is not personal data "to the extent that [it] cannot be related to identified or identifiable living individuals" [23]; the cross-jurisdictional review of synthetic-data guidance confirms that generation is itself processing of personal data requiring a lawful basis, with the output qualifying as non-personal only when residual re-identification risk is sufficiently remote [24]; Singapore's PDPC adds that synthetic data is not inherently risk-free [25]. The practical conclusion is sharp: the synthetic nature of the codes is legally irrelevant while the paired text remains attributable. The text field dominates the pair's status, which is why the content transformation is the lane's actual privacy work.

## Anonymisation chain as regulated processing

The guidelines are explicit that anonymisation is processing: the principles of data protection apply to "processing operations carried out to obtain anonymous information", which must have an Article 6 lawful basis, plus an Article 9(2) condition where special-category data is involved, presumed to coincide with the basis of the preceding processing when part of the same activity [1][21]. Three operational duties follow for the lane. Transparency obligations attach to the anonymisation processing itself, and controllers "should clearly state that personal data will be processed to produce anonymous data which then falls outside of the scope of the GDPR" while avoiding ambiguous or misleading statements - in particular, they must not describe data as "anonymous", "de-identified" or "de-personalised" if individuals are actually still identifiable [1]. Documentation of the anonymisation process, including testing of the supposedly anonymous datasets, must be retained after completion [1]. And the EDPS v SRB reasoning embedded in the guidelines' Example 2 fixes the timing: information duties are assessed at collection from the controller's perspective, so the processing chain must be disclosed when the audio is captured, even though what eventually leaves the device is anonymous [1].

One design property of the lane gets explicit regulatory credit: immediate anonymisation combined with immediate deletion of the original data limits the impact on data subjects and acts as a positive factor in the legitimate-interest balancing test [1]. Discarding the original voice right after transcription is therefore not just good hygiene - it is a recognized mitigation that strengthens the lawful-basis position for the chain.

The US biometric layer runs in parallel and is worth one paragraph because it treats voice differently from text. Illinois BIPA enumerates "voiceprint" as a biometric identifier and requires written notice of purpose and duration, a written release before collection, and a publicly available retention-and-destruction schedule [26][27]. The statutory trigger is the creation of an identifying template from vocal characteristics rather than the mere recording of audio [28], but litigation theories are expansive: the Apple Siri BIPA class was certified on 29 January 2026 on allegations that included feature vectors computed during speech decoding for all Siri users, not only those enrolled in voice recognition [29]. Enforcement is active - a proposed class action over an AI phone system's voice capture was filed in July 2026 [26] - and the 2024 amendment capped but preserved per-collection recovery with electronic signatures counting as written releases [27]. The lane's strongest BIPA posture is structural: never store voice, never compute or store speaker embeddings, delete the waveform immediately, publish a retention-and-destruction schedule, name voice processing explicitly in the consent receipt, and never monetize the voice itself. Under the Siri theory, even transient feature extraction during ASR is alleged to be collection, so the consent and disclosure duties apply regardless; what the lane eliminates is retention, which is where the damages accumulate.

## Precedents from voice-assistant data programs

No shipping product runs the proposed transcribe-transform-resynthesize lane, and the two closest precedents sit on opposite sides of it. After the 2019 disclosure that contractors had been grading Siri recordings, Apple suspended the program, apologized, and announced that by default it would no longer retain audio of Siri interactions; human listening resumed only on opt-in, performed by employees; computer-generated transcripts continued to be used to improve Siri, associated with a random identifier rather than an Apple ID for up to six months, with no opt-out short of disabling Siri entirely [30][31][32]. Apple's settlement of the scandal is instructive: it kept text and dropped audio. The proposed lane keeps transformed text plus synthetic codes and drops audio - a different point on the same axis, and one that preserves training utility for speech output rather than only recognition.

Common Voice is the other pole: contributors knowingly donate real voices, released publicly under CC0 with optional demographics, with clips linked across the dataset by a hashed client_id, demographics stripped for languages with fewer than five speakers, and clips removed only at user request [33][34]. That is a real-voice consent lane, pseudonymous by construction and dependent on explicit donation. It demonstrates what a consent receipt for voice data must enumerate, and it demonstrates what the NO-KEY invariant rejects: a persistent contributor identifier that makes every clip linkable. The proposed lane is novel relative to both precedents, which means its disclosure cannot borrow existing wording and must be self-explanatory.

## Lane design conditions

The eight conditions below compose into the viable version of the lane. Each is independently motivated by the evidence above; together they close the gaps the evidence exposes.

The table states each condition, its requirement, and the evidence behind it.

| Condition | Requirement | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| C1 Scrub-before-synthesize | Deterministic PII scrub first, then style-transforming paraphrase with a local model, then Talker synthesis from the transformed text; the pair stays aligned for training | NER masking alone fails; paraphrase cuts re-identification attacks by more than 50%; content attacks defeat untransformed ASR-TTS output [5][7] |
| C2 No user-audio conditioning | Talker input is transformed text plus the fixed preset voice embedding only; no F0, duration, or emotion passthrough; voice cloning stays forbidden for Route B data generation | Prosody and emotion carry measured identity; VC leakage is exactly this path [4][9][10] |
| C3 No-key pooling | No device, session, or contributor identifier anywhere in the pipeline; session stamps stripped before retention; batches shuffled before local training and before egress | NO-KEY invariant; the guidelines' transfer rule keeps data personal if any downstream entity could have means to identify [1] |
| C4 Adversarial evaluation gate | Run attribute-inference and authorship attacks locally against transformed text; drop failing records; version the battery as a tuning knob | Best single-pass anonymizers leave roughly 27% residual re-identification risk; adversarial loops are the current best defense; stylometric outliers are the highest-risk records [35][36][37] |
| C5 Memorization control | Train only on transformed content, deduplicate, and gate released models against extraction and regurgitation | Opinion 28/2024 extraction/query test; guidelines para 82 on prompting synthetic models; TTS membership-inference evidence [22][1][38] |
| C6 Immediate-deletion semantics | Original voice discarded after transcription, before transformation; deletion logged in pipeline telemetry | Positive factor in the legitimate-interest balancing test; BIPA retention duties [1][27] |
| C7 Disclosure at capture | Versioned consent receipt names every stage of the chain; never label the data "anonymous" while content remains attributable | Guidelines para 40 wording rule; EDPS v SRB collection-time assessment; Siri disclosure failure [1][30] |
| C8 Irrevocability disclosure | State that contributions cannot be individually removed once past the no-key pooling boundary | Relative approach plus NO-KEY; contrast with Common Voice's pre-release removal only [34] |

The conditions are mutually dependent in ways worth stating. C1 without C4 ships content that measurably resists attack only probabilistically; C4 without C3 still lets an attacker aggregate a single user's records within a batch by style, because the guidelines' No Linkage criterion looks across datasets [1]; C5 without C1 trains models on identifiable content and then relies on the model not to leak it, which the membership-inference literature says is a losing bet for ASR and TTS systems alike [39][40]. C6 is what converts the whole construction from pseudonymization into the strongest available posture for the acoustic channel, and it is the property the disclosure should lead with.

## Disclosure wording

The consent receipt must enumerate the chain at collection time, in the user's language, versioned with the acceptance recorded as a receipt. A draft for legal review:

"When you use voice mode, your speech is transcribed on this device and the recording is deleted immediately. Before anything is kept, the transcript is rewritten to remove personal details and your individual turns of phrase, and the rewritten text is re-synthesized in a synthetic voice that is not yours. The resulting pairs of rewritten text and synthetic-voice codes may be used to improve speech models on this device and, in batches containing no device, account, or contributor identifiers, may be published to improve speech models elsewhere. Your voice is never stored and never leaves your device."

Three wording rules constrain it. Do not claim the data is anonymous while any component remains attributable to an individual - the guidelines prohibit "anonymous", "de-identified" and "de-personalised" labels in that state [1]. Do not bury the voice clause inside generic terms; BIPA requires the biometric collection to be specifically disclosed, and the Siri episode shows that undisclosed voice processing is what converts a data program into a scandal and a class action [30][29]. And because the wording describes a processing chain the vendor must actually run, the disclosure doubles as the binding contract for what the pipeline is permitted to do - a version change in the pipeline requires a version change in the receipt.

## Residual risks and open items

The content channel's risk is managed, not proven away, and the honest posture is the guidelines' own: documented residual risk under the contextual approach, with safety margins and periodic reassessment, rather than a one-time absolutist claim [1]. Single-pass anonymization by the best available tools leaves roughly 27% re-identification success against LLM attackers in benchmark evaluation [35]; famous or highly public individuals are disproportionately exposed because auxiliary public data makes matching trivial [41]; and agentic re-identification improves with every additional attributable record, which is why per-user contribution caps per published batch belong in the battery design [6][17].

Model memorization is the second open front. Membership inference works against speech models - through alignment losses in TTS and at the user level against ASR - and extraction attacks against language models are a mature literature [38][39][40]. Condition C5's mitigation (train on transformed content, deduplicate, audit before release) is standard, but the audit battery for the shipped drafter does not exist yet and should be built as part of Route B's release gate, not after.

One timing note: Guidelines 02/2026 are version 1.0 in consultation until 30 October 2026, and the final text may tighten or clarify the criteria [2]. The lane is deliberately built on the framework's durable parts - the three criteria, the further-analysis posture, the process-compliance duties - rather than on any reading that could not survive revision.

The closing judgment is the one the design documents should carry: the Talker can release the voice in the pair, but not the words. The lane holds if the words are transformed too, if cloning stays forbidden, if pooling stays keyless, and if the disclosure names the whole chain. Name it accordingly - a voice-release lane, with anonymity as the property the content layer has to earn and re-earn, record by record.
